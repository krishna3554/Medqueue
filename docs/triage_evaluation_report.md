# MedQueueAI Triage Evaluation Report

**Status:** Draft engineering evaluation; pending clinical review  
**Model version:** `synthetic-v0.1`  
**Purpose:** Document the dataset, model, triage architecture, evaluation metrics, queue outcomes, comparisons, limitations, and conclusions for the MedQueueAI prototype.

> **Safety statement:** MedQueueAI is decision-support software for a prototype. It is not a diagnostic or clinical-care system. The model and all reported performance results use synthetic data only. These results must not be interpreted as evidence of clinical validity, safety, or readiness for deployment.

## 1. Executive summary

MedQueueAI uses a rules-first triage pipeline. Configured red-flag rules are evaluated before any machine-learning output. A matching red flag is assigned Level 1. If no rule matches, the optional urgency model predicts a level from 1 to 5 and generates an explanation. If model artifacts are unavailable or fail, the service falls back to a deterministic stub so registration and queue operation continue.

The selected candidate is an XGBoost classifier, version `synthetic-v0.1`. On a fresh synthetic evaluation set of 3,000 records it achieved:

| Metric | Result |
|---|---:|
| One-vs-rest AUC | **0.963** |
| Recall for urgent Levels 1–2 | **0.913** |
| Level 1 recall | 0.856 |
| Level 2 recall | 0.949 |
| Cohen’s kappa | **0.881** |
| Accuracy within ±1 triage level | **0.997** |

The strongest operational outcome came from severity-aware queueing rather than FCFS: mean red-flag time-to-first-review fell from **58.18 minutes** under FCFS to **3.83 minutes** under both severity-only and MedQueueAI policies in the final simulation. This improvement came with a major trade-off: lower-acuity Levels 4–5 experienced substantially longer waits under severity-aware policies.

The most important conclusion is therefore not “the model is accurate enough for care.” It is that the prototype demonstrates a technically coherent rules-first, explainable, severity-aware workflow whose synthetic results justify further clinical validation—not deployment.

## 2. Dataset used

### 2.1 Triage model dataset

No real patient data is used. No public emergency-department triage dataset was used. The project notes that MIMIC-IV-ED requires credentialed access, so the model dataset is generated by [`ml/synthetic.py`](../ml/synthetic.py).

Training data:

- 6,000 synthetic records generated with seed `42`.
- 75% train / 25% internal test split, stratified by triage level.
- A separate evaluation run generates 3,000 fresh synthetic records with seed `123`.
- Labels are generated from transparent rule logic, then 10% of labels are shifted by ±1 level to avoid a perfectly separable task.

### 2.2 Synthetic population and features

Age distribution:

| Group | Approximate share |
|---|---:|
| Infant, under 2 | 5% |
| Child, 2–17 | 15% |
| Adult, 18–64 | 60% |
| Older adult, 65+ | 20% |

Symptoms are sampled independently in the generator, with engineered correlations between symptoms and abnormal vitals. The 14 symptom indicators are:

`chest_pain`, `chest_tightness`, `breathlessness`, `fever`, `cough`, `abdominal_pain`, `dizziness`, `confusion`, `unresponsive`, `seizure`, `facial_droop`, `slurred_speech`, `one_sided_weakness`, and `heavy_bleeding`.

Vitals are:

- SpO₂: Normal(97, 2), clipped to 70–100
- Pulse: Normal(82, 14), clipped to 30–180
- Systolic blood pressure: Normal(122, 18), clipped to 60–240
- Diastolic blood pressure: Normal(78, 12), clipped to 30–140
- Temperature: Normal(37.0, 0.8), clipped to 34–42 °C

Missingness is deliberately simulated: SpO₂ and pulse 8% each, systolic blood pressure 10%, diastolic blood pressure 15%, and temperature 8%.

The model uses 31 features:

- 14 symptom flags
- age, age band, and sex
- five vital values
- shock index (`pulse / sbp`)
- fever flag and hypoxia flag
- one missingness indicator for each vital

Missing numerical values are imputed with training-set medians. Missingness indicators are retained as features.

### 2.3 Synthetic label construction

The generator creates five urgency levels:

- **Level 1:** red-flag patterns such as severe hypoxia, extreme blood pressure, altered consciousness, stroke signs, severe bleeding, or high fever at age extremes.
- **Level 2:** high fever, high pulse, or chest/breathlessness presentations with abnormal vitals.
- **Level 3:** high systolic blood pressure or abdominal pain/dizziness presentations.
- **Level 4:** mild symptoms such as fever, cough, abdominal pain, or dizziness.
- **Level 5:** no symptoms with normal vitals.

Ten percent of generated labels are shifted by one level, clipped to the valid range 1–5. Because the labels are generated from rules, the evaluation measures agreement with an engineered label function, not agreement with clinician judgment or patient outcomes.

### 2.4 Separate NLP dataset

Symptom extraction is evaluated separately from urgency triage:

- 120 utterances in `backend/tests/data/utterances.csv`
- English, Hindi Devanagari, Romanised Hindi, Marathi, and code-switched text
- Negation and duration examples included
- Draft vocabulary of 150 canonical symptoms in `docs/vocabulary.csv`

NLP results were TP=118, FP=17, FN=14, precision=0.874, and recall=0.894. These are extraction metrics, not triage-model metrics.

## 3. Triage architecture

```text
Confirmed symptoms + latest vitals + patient age
                         |
                         v
              Red-flag rule evaluation
                    |            |
             match /            \ no match
                  v              v
        Level 1, source=rules   ML urgency model
                                      |       |
                              available /   \ unavailable or error
                                    v          v
                         Level 1–5, model   deterministic stub
                         explanation        source=stub
```

### 3.1 Rules layer

The rule configuration is [`docs/red_flag_rules.yaml`](red_flag_rules.yaml), version 0.1. Current rules cover:

| ID | Rule |
|---|---|
| RF01 | Chest pain/tightness with SpO₂ < 92 |
| RF02 | Chest pain/tightness with breathlessness |
| RF03 | SpO₂ < 90 |
| RF04 | Systolic blood pressure ≥ 200 or < 80 |
| RF05 | Confusion, unresponsiveness, or seizure |
| RF06 | Facial droop, slurred speech, or one-sided weakness |
| RF07 | Heavy bleeding |
| RF08 | Temperature ≥ 39 °C with age < 2 or ≥ 75 |

Unknown vitals do not count as normal. Rules are always evaluated before the model and take precedence over model output.

### 3.2 ML layer

The training code evaluates three candidates:

- Logistic Regression
- Random Forest
- XGBoost

All candidates use stratified data, class balancing, median imputation, and calibrated probabilities. Selection prioritizes recall for Levels 1–2, then interpretability. The selected artifact is XGBoost.

Model explanations are generated using SHAP top-three feature contributions, with a deterministic deviation-based fallback if SHAP is unavailable. Explanations are engineering rationales, not clinical reasons.

### 3.3 Fallback layer

If model files or model dependencies are unavailable, the API does not block registration. It uses the deterministic stub:

- temperature ≥ 38.5 °C or pulse ≥ 110 → Level 2
- systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 → Level 3
- otherwise → Level 4

Red-flag rules still run first in the fallback path.

### 3.4 Human oversight

Clinical users can override the automated level. The override replaces the base level for queue priority, preserves the automated level, and records the actor, reason, and timestamp in the audit log.

### 5.4 What “accuracy” means here

The project reports **99.7% within-±1-level accuracy**. This does not mean 99.7% exact classification accuracy. Exact-match accuracy is not included in the current evaluation artifact and should not be inferred from the within-one-level measure. The urgent recall of 91.3% is the more relevant reported measure for the prototype’s stated priority, but it is still measured against synthetic labels.

## 6. Queue and operational outcomes

The simulation compares three dispatch techniques:

1. **FCFS:** earliest registration served first.
2. **Severity-only:** lowest triage level number served first.
3. **MedQueueAI:** weighted urgency + waiting-time aging + red-flag boost.

Simulation setup:

- 30 random seeds for final comparison
- 2 doctors
- 480-minute operating horizon plus drain time
- Bursty arrivals, lognormal consult durations, doctor breaks
- 10 patients/hour base arrival rate
- Urgency mix: L1 5%, L2 15%, L3 25%, L4 30%, L5 25%
- Synthetic evidence only

### 6.1 Final comparison

Values are means with ±95% confidence intervals where reported.

| Policy | Red-flag mean time to first review | Max L4–5 wait | Utilisation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| FCFS | 58.18 ± 16.49 min | 120.78 ± 21.04 min | 0.76 ± 0.03 |
| Severity-only | 3.83 ± 0.75 min | 293.91 ± 46.69 min | 0.76 ± 0.03 |
| MedQueueAI | 3.83 ± 0.75 min | 284.58 ± 47.47 min | 0.76 ± 0.03 |

### 6.2 Interpretation

- Severity-aware dispatch reduced synthetic red-flag time to first review by approximately 54.35 minutes versus FCFS, an approximately 93% reduction.
- MedQueueAI matched severity-only performance for Level 1 red-flag response in this simulation.
- MedQueueAI slightly reduced maximum Level 4–5 wait versus severity-only: 284.58 versus 293.91 minutes, about 9.33 minutes lower on average.
- Both severity-aware policies increased low-acuity waiting substantially compared with FCFS. This is a starvation/fairness trade-off, not an unqualified improvement.
- Utilisation and number served were effectively unchanged across policies in the final comparison, so the main effect was redistribution of waiting time by urgency.

The implementation’s queue defaults are `w_u=0.65`, `w_w=0.30`, red-flag boost `w_f=1.0`, and aging reference `w_ref_min=60`. Synthetic grid search selected `w_u=0.5`, `w_w=0.15`, `w_ref_min=60`, but the code retained its safer review-pending defaults rather than changing production behavior based only on synthetic data.

## 7. Comparison with existing techniques

### 7.1 Triage model baselines

The project compares the selected XGBoost model with Logistic Regression and Random Forest. XGBoost performs best on the internal synthetic comparison for urgent recall, kappa, and within-one-level accuracy. This is an internal baseline comparison; there is no comparison with a validated external clinical triage model or real-world benchmark.

### 7.2 Queue baselines

Compared with FCFS, severity-aware queueing provides much faster synthetic red-flag review but can delay lower-acuity patients. Compared with severity-only ordering, MedQueueAI adds waiting-time aging and a red-flag boost to reduce the worst low-acuity waits while preserving urgent response in the tested scenario.

### 7.3 What is not established

The current work does not establish superiority over:

- a validated hospital triage scale,
- clinician triage decisions,
- a real EHR or emergency-department dataset,
- a production queue-management system,
- outcomes such as mortality, admission, complications, or patient satisfaction.

## 8. Key outcomes

1. A complete rules-first triage architecture is implemented.
2. Red flags are protected from model errors by precedence rules.
3. The model achieved strong synthetic discrimination and urgent recall, with 0.963 AUC and 0.913 Level 1–2 recall on the fresh synthetic evaluation set.
4. The model’s Level 1 recall is 0.856, so even in synthetic evaluation it misses some generated Level 1 cases; this is not an acceptable clinical safety estimate.
5. SHAP-based top-three factors and deterministic fallbacks make model output inspectable.
6. Registration remains available when AI artifacts are missing.
7. Severity-aware queueing substantially improves synthetic red-flag response compared with FCFS.
8. Severity-aware queueing creates a serious low-acuity wait-time trade-off that requires capacity planning and clinical review.
9. Symptom NLP supports multilingual intake, but its draft precision (0.874) and recall (0.894) require native-speaker and clinical review.
10. Human overrides, reasons, and audit events are part of the workflow.

## 9. Limitations and risks

### Data and labels

- All records and labels are synthetic.
- Distributions are engineered for plausibility, not estimated from epidemiology.
- The labels are generated from rules similar to the serving logic, creating a favorable relationship between features and labels.
- Ten-percent label noise does not reproduce real annotation disagreement.
- Simulated missingness is not equivalent to clinical documentation missingness.
- No outcome labels, clinician adjudication, hospital-specific population, or external validation are present.

### Model and evaluation

- The fresh test set is generated by the same synthetic generator family as training.
- Exact accuracy, confusion matrices, sensitivity/specificity by clinically defined endpoint, and prospective calibration are not currently reported.
- The reported urgent recall is not a clinical sensitivity estimate.
- SHAP explanations can be unstable or misleading and are not clinical reasoning.
- Model binaries are optional and may be unavailable at runtime, activating the stub fallback.

### Queue and fairness

- Simulation results depend on two doctors, arrival bursts, consult-time assumptions, breaks, and the selected urgency mix.
- Lower-acuity patients can experience very long waits under severity-aware policies.
- Age-band recall is descriptive only; it is not a fairness or safety audit.
- No analysis is provided for sex, language, disability, socioeconomic status, access, or other protected or operational groups.

### Clinical safety

- Thresholds and rule definitions are drafts requiring clinician sign-off.
- The software must not be used to determine care priority without an approved clinical governance process.
- A red-flag match should be treated as an escalation signal for trained staff, not as a diagnosis.

## 10. Conclusions and recommended next steps

### Conclusions

The prototype provides evidence that a rules-first triage service, combined with an explainable synthetic urgency model and waiting-time-aware queue, can be implemented consistently and tested end-to-end. Synthetic model results are strong, and simulations show that severity-aware dispatch can reduce red-flag review delay dramatically compared with FCFS.

Those findings are engineering outcomes only. They do not demonstrate clinical accuracy, safety, fairness, or generalisation. The most concerning observed trade-off is prolonged waiting for Levels 4–5 under bursty demand.

### Required before any clinical use

1. Obtain an approved, representative, de-identified clinical dataset with clinician-adjudicated triage labels and relevant outcomes.
2. Have clinicians review and approve every red-flag rule, symptom definition, vital threshold, queue weight, and override policy.
3. Define a clinical evaluation protocol with a locked test set, external validation, confidence intervals, calibration, confusion matrices, urgent-case sensitivity, and missed-red-flag review.
4. Evaluate subgroup performance and operational fairness across age, sex, language, disability, and other relevant groups.
5. Test the queue with realistic hospital arrival patterns, doctor capacity, breaks, reassignment, cancellations, and service-level constraints.
6. Add safeguards for model/version drift, stale rules, unavailable artifacts, data-quality failures, and audit review.
7. Conduct silent-mode prospective validation before any workflow influence.
8. Establish a human-in-the-loop escalation process and document that clinicians retain final responsibility.
